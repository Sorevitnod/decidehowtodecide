The phrases that
close the room.

These can be fair questions or real constraints. Used as conversation stoppers, they end the inquiry just when it matters most. Try the next question instead.

“I own this decision.”

Then ask: “Whose knowledge do I need before I make it?”

“That’s my red line.”

Then ask: “What makes it non-negotiable, and what is still open?”

“We need a simple answer.”

Then ask: “What must we keep in view so the answer stays useful?”

“This feels academic. When do we get to practical delivery?”

Then ask: “What can we try now, and what assumption would that test?”

A deadline, an owner, a hard boundary or a simpler explanation may be exactly what the situation needs. Holding certainty lightly means explaining the reason and leaving room to test the parts that are still uncertain.

The manifesto

Five ways to
think better together.

Hold certainty lightly: make your view clear, then leave room for information that could change it.

01 /

Name the constraint.
Don’t weaponise it.

If something is fixed, explain why, who set it, and what remains open. “My red line” tells people very little until you make the reason visible.

02 /

Own the decision.
Invite the challenge.

Accountability identifies who makes the call. It does not identify whose knowledge matters. Make it safe to question an assumption before the choice becomes a commitment. The evidence ↗

03 /

Watch for the rush
to closure.

A clear answer feels good, especially under pressure. Ask what evidence you stopped seeking when the first plausible answer appeared. The evidence ↗

04 /

Simplify the work.
Keep the important complexity.

We have limited attention. Use a tool to make the tradeoffs manageable, then check which differences the simplification erased. The evidence ↗

05 /

Move the sliders.
Then decide.

Urgency, reversibility, risk and participation change the right process. Make a choice you can explain, and revisit it when the conditions change.

Life is full of sliders.

Holding certainty lightly means being clear about what you believe—and equally clear about what might change your mind.

The thinking behind it

Provocative words. Careful claims.

This manifesto draws on research into psychological safety, the need for cognitive closure, cognitive limits and group decision making. The research supports better questions and practices; it does not turn any one slogan or slider setting into a scientific rule.
